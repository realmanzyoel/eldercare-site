The short answer

Refusing help is common and usually comes from fear of losing independence, not stubbornness. It helps to introduce help slowly, call it something else (a friend, a cleaner, someone 'for you, not them'), offer choices, and let the support worker start with something they enjoy.

Why they say no

  • They do not believe they need help, which is part of the illness for many people
  • Fear of strangers, of being 'put in a home' or of the cost
  • Embarrassment about washing and toileting
  • Feeling talked about rather than talked to

Approaches that work

  • Start small: a 3-hour visit for an outing, cooking or gardening, not 'care'
  • Introduce the support worker as a friend, a helper for you, or someone the doctor suggested
  • Offer two choices, both of which are yes: 'Tuesday or Thursday?'
  • Keep the same person for the first weeks
  • Leave personal care until trust is built, unless safety demands it
  • Let them give something back: teaching a recipe, showing photos

Avoid long explanations and arguments. If a visit goes badly, try again another day with a different activity. Most people settle within two to four weeks.

If they are unsafe and still refuse all help, speak to their doctor and the National Dementia Helpline on 1800 100 500 about options.
